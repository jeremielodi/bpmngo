                                                      Abstract
          This report analyzes the supplied IT-helpdesk process and presents a possible redesign. The analytical
      as-is cycle-time efficiency is 4.742%, with a processing labor cost of €176.67 per execution under Exercise
      1’s handoff exclusion. An Erlang-C calculation requires three Level-2 employees for a two-hour resolution
      service at one request per hour; including evaluation and writing in a single service visit requires four.
      At the four-request-per-hour workload of Exercise 3, three Level-2 employees are insufficient. A 12-
      configuration simulation identifies the capacity bottleneck and diminishing returns beyond approximately
      12 Level-2 employees. Six issues are linked to redesign proposals. The selected to-be model automates
      routine delivery and feedback routing while retaining human assistance. At matched staffing, the main
      redesign run reduces modeled labor cost per case by approximately 7%; its smaller cycle-time change
      requires more cautious interpretation.


Scope and modeling conventions
The assignment describes five Level-1 employees at €40 per person-hour and three Level-2 employees at
€60 per person-hour, serving approximately 500 company employees [1]. Each staffed activity requires one
employee. The capacity of three is a shared pool, not a three-person team per request.
The supplied BPMN model is used for Exercises 1 and 3. Known requests occur with probability 0.2; otherwise
the request is escalated. Each delivered Level-2 resolution succeeds with probability 0.8. Unsuccessful cases
return to resolution work, not to initial evaluation. Repeated outcomes are modeled as independent with
unchanged probabilities. No business-hour calendar is imposed because the assignment provides no shift
schedule. All reported hours are continuous modeled hours unless explicitly identified as working hours in
the queueing calculation.

2.2    Sensitivity to the service boundary
If “Level-2 service” includes the initial 20-minute evaluation and 20-minute writing task, one visit lasts
(20 + 120 + 20)/60 = 2.667 hours. With the same M/M/c approximation, three staff then yield Wq = 6.380
hours; four yield 0.757 hours. This alternative excludes repeat visits. If one request per hour means new
requests only, with rework generating additional visits, the arrival/workload model must also account for
that feedback. The two staffing answers therefore correspond to different explicit service boundaries; the
assignment’s mean times alone do not uniquely specify a queueing model.


3     Exercise 3: simulation and resource experiments

3.1    Experiment design
BpmnGo’s discrete-event simulator was configured with constant Poisson demand at four requests per hour,
a 720-hour admission horizon, 20 replications, and initial seed 42 (replication seeds 42–61). The system
starts empty; no warm-up observations are discarded. Resources are continuously available, use a common
queue per pool, and are released between activities. Individual staff identities are not retained across a case’s
successive tasks.
All fixed internal waiting times in Table 1 are set to zero. Only the client-response delay remains, as an
unresourced 1200-minute wait. Waiting for an occupied staff pool is generated by the simulation, not inserted
as another fixed delay. Forwarding unsuccessful feedback is modeled here as two minutes of Level-1 work.
Exercise 1’s special handoff exclusion is not applied to this operational simulation.
The known-resolution communication time follows a normal distribution with mean 5 and standard deviation
3 minutes; post-writing communication uses mean 20 and standard deviation 5. Other processing durations
are fixed at the supplied means. BpmnGo resamples negative normal draws, so the realized mean of the
short activity is slightly higher than the nominal five minutes. This limitation also explains small differences
between simulated and analytical labor costs.
The baseline uses 5 Level-1 and 3 Level-2 staff. The factorial campaign compares Level-1 capacities {3, 5}
and Level-2 capacities {3, 6, 9, 11, 12, 15}: 12 configurations, each with 20 replications. Two separate detailed
runs retain the baseline and the 5/12 configuration for resource-pressure comparison.


3.2    Measured results
The baseline mean cycle is 1063.69 hours, with an approximate 95% confidence interval of 1047.12–1080.26
hours across replication means. Level-2 utilization is 97.37%, its mean queue is 1166.92 requests, and the
mean of per-replication maximum queues is 1935. At 5/12, mean cycle falls to 23.81 hours (95% interval
23.68–23.93 hours), Level-2 utilization is 78.32%, and its mean queue falls to 2.25 requests. These metrics
include the period after arrivals stop while the remaining workload is drained.

3.3    Interpretation and trade-offs
The bottleneck can also be established analytically. Expected Level-2 work per incoming request is 156
minutes, so at four arrivals per hour the offered workload is
                                              156
                                aL2 = 4 ×         = 10.4 staff-hours per hour.
                                               60
Three, six and nine Level-2 employees cannot sustain this workload. Eleven is the smallest integer capacity
strictly above it, but has little spare capacity. The results show diminishing returns after approximately 12
Level-2 staff: with five Level-1 staff, increasing Level-2 capacity from 12 to 15 saves about 39 minutes of
mean cycle time while increasing the hourly staffing budget from €920 to €1100.
Client feedback becomes dominant once staff queues are small. The expected number of feedback waits per
incoming request is 0.8/(1 − 0.2) = 1, contributing 20 hours. With approximately 3.1 hours of work, the
no-resource-queue analytical reference is near 23.1 hours. Merely adding more employees cannot remove this
external delay.
The 3/12 configuration gives a mean of 23.89 hours at €840 per staffed hour, compared with 23.81 hours
and €920 at 5/12. It is a candidate for further assessment, not proof of equivalent service: the factorial
output does not retain an uncertainty interval for the difference, and no service-level target or budget was
specified. BpmnGo’s selected 5/15 configuration minimizes mean cycle among the tested points; it is not a
demonstrated economic optimum.


3.4    Metric limitations
Every admitted case is drained to completion. Consequently, a completed campaign does not imply a stable
queue: the overloaded baseline is a finite-batch result, not a steady-state waiting-time estimate. Throughput
divides completions by elapsed time through the final completion, and utilization uses the same extended
period. This explains why adequately staffed runs can report fewer than four completions per hour. Counts
are integer averages per replication, approximately 2869 cases, not totals across all √ replications. Reported
p95 values average per-replication percentiles. Confidence intervals use x̄ ± 1.96s/ 20.
Labor cost per case remains around €177 because staffing mainly changes waiting, not the amount of work.
It excludes idle payroll. The separate budget measure is 40cL1 + 60cL2 per hour. Equal initial seeds improve
reproducibility but do not ensure identical case paths across configurations: changes in event order alter
random-draw consumption. Small differences in costs and paths should therefore not be interpreted as
redesign benefits.

The following proposals apply the redesign heuristic terminology of Reijers and Limam Mansar [2]. Their
helpdesk-specific justification and test plans are developed here.

C1 — Extra resources (I1). Validate sufficient qualified Level-2 capacity, using 12 as a candidate under
    the original workload. The measured 5/12 result directly addresses queue growth, but raises the hourly
    staffing budget. Reassess capacity after changing work or routing; additional employees should not
    substitute for analyzing unnecessary work.
C2 — Task automation (I2). Introduce event-driven assignment and notification to reduce avoidable
    pickup and polling delay. First measure dispatch latency separately from capacity waiting: the
    assignment does not identify how much of each stated delay is attributable to batch handling rather
    than staff shortage. Use a separate timing sensitivity study for the original fixed delays; an alert cannot
    remove a genuine resource queue.
C3 — Task elimination and task automation (I3). Deliver prepared resolutions automatically and
    route unsuccessful client feedback directly to Level-2. Retain human assistance for cases requiring
    explanation. This targets duplicate relaying rather than diagnosis. Test labor and cycle effects at
    unchanged staffing and probabilities, and account for automation costs before claiming financial savings.
C4 — Specialist–generalist (I4). Broaden Level-1 competence through targeted training and a main-
    tained knowledge base, retaining escalation for difficult cases. Test known/unknown probabilities
    of 0.2/0.8, 0.3/0.7 and 0.4/0.6 as paired scenarios. With other assumptions fixed, a 40% known
    rate would reduce Level-2 workload to 7.8 staff-hours/hour. This is a conditional calculation, not an
    observed improvement; training and additional checking effort, and the risk of incorrect closure, must
    be included.
C5 — Control addition (I5). Add proportionate output validation before delivery. Test check durations
    of 0, 5 and 10 minutes together with explicitly hypothesized failure rates of 0.20, 0.15 and 0.10. Reduced
    rework may offset the extra effort, but checking can also worsen an overloaded Level-2 queue. Validate
    quality and total labor, rather than lowering the failure probability without paying for the proposed
    control.
C6 — Integration and task automation (I6). Provide direct test-result submission, clear instructions
    and proportionate reminders. Test 20-, 10- and 5-hour client-response delays as sensitivity scenarios,
    then measure actual behavior in a pilot. Client availability remains external to the desk; silence must
    not be treated as successful resolution.

A practical sequence is to validate capacity, instrument dispatch, simplify the relay, then pilot skill, quality
and feedback improvements. C3 and C6 can share a communication channel. Staffing must be recalculated
after interventions change workload. Six detailed entries, with evidence, heuristics, justification, trade-offs
and validation plans, were persisted in BpmnGo’s issue register.

5.1    Selected changes
Figure 1 implements a subset of C2, C3 and C6. The model preserves request checking, the known-resolution
branch, initial Level-2 evaluation, problem solving and writing. A new exclusive gateway selects either
automated notification or Level-1 assisted delivery. Both paths merge before receiving the client’s test result.
Confirmed successes close; failures are routed automatically back to the merge before problem solving. Initial
evaluation is not repeated.
The lanes separate Level-1 work, Level-2 work, and workflow automation/client-response handling. The
receive task represents the desk waiting for external feedback; the client’s internal testing process is outside
the diagram. The design was imported into BpmnGo’s modeler, exported as editable BPMN, and saved as
a separate simulation project. Service-task symbols describe proposed integrations, not already deployed
notification or routing services.


5.2    Additional assumptions and matched comparison
The main scenario assumes 90% of post-writing deliveries can be automated and 10% require human
assistance. This new probability is illustrative, not supplied by the assignment. The assistance flag is
assumed available without an additional assessment task and is sampled at each delivery attempt. The
assisted path retains the original normal communication duration (mean 20 min, standard deviation 5 min).
Both automated tasks take one minute, require no human resource, and have zero modeled incremental cost;
development and operating costs remain unestimated.
Known/unknown probabilities remain 0.2/0.8, solved/rework probabilities remain 0.8/0.2, and client waiting
remains 1200 minutes. No benefit from faster client responses, better first-level resolution or fewer failures
is assumed. The primary as-is and to-be runs both use 5/12 staffing, four arrivals per hour, 720 hours, 20
replications and seed 42. Additional runs test 0% and 100% automated delivery at 5/12 and 90% automation
at 5/3.

Overall assessment
The original process combines a capacity bottleneck, repeated work and substantial external waiting. Staffing
changes have a large effect where capacity is inadequate; process simplification reduces routine labor but
does not replace specialist work. The selected to-be model is a coherent proposal with explicit exceptions and
unchanged outcome probabilities. Its operational adoption would require validation of automation coverage,
communication quality, service targets and implementation costs. The issue register remains open because
simulation is evidence for a design proposal, not confirmation of a production improvement.
